Module 1

Many Robots, One Garage

Modern projects don’t have one AI session working at a time — they have several, in parallel, all editing the same repository. One session is building a feature, another is fixing a bug, a third is refreshing the docs. Picture several mechanics sharing one garage: each rolls the car onto their own lift, works on their own part — and then everyone’s changes have to fit back onto the same car.

Git makes the “own lift” part easy: each session works on its own branch, inside its own worktree. The trouble starts when the parallel work has to come back together. This module is about the three ways it goes wrong — and why two of the three are invisible until it’s too late.

Watch two sessions collide

Here are two well-behaved AI sessions, each doing exactly what it was asked, and the shared main branch caught in the middle. Neither session did anything wrong — and everything still goes wrong. Play the conversation:

A
Session A

Starting my task! I branched off main on Tuesday. Clean copy, all tests green.

B
Session B

Me too! I also need a number for my new spec folder. The highest one I can see is 051, so I’ll take 052.

A
Session A

Funny — the highest number I can see is also 051. Taking 052 for mine.

M
Main branch

Heads up: you have now BOTH created a folder called spec 052 — with completely different contents. I will happily accept both. No error. Good luck to whoever reads the specs later.

B
Session B

Done with my feature! Merging into main. Four files changed.

A
Session A

Wait. My branch suddenly conflicts with main in two files. I did not touch anything since yesterday!

M
Main branch

You did not move — I did. Your branch is based on Tuesday’s me. Session B just changed two of the files you also changed. The conflict was born the moment B merged; you are only discovering it now.

?

Three distinct failures hide in that exchange. A merge conflict: two sessions edited the same files, and the second one to arrive inherits the mess. A stolen spec number: both sessions claimed “052”, and because the folders have different names, git raises no error at all — the collision is silent. And a stale checkout: Session A was reasoning about Tuesday’s version of the project while the real one moved on without it.

⚠️
The nasty part: two of the three are silent

Git loudly flags a merge conflict — eventually. But it says nothing about two different spec folders sharing a number, and nothing about a branch quietly going stale while main moves. The failures you aren’t warned about are the ones that cost days.

The rules of the road (before this task)

The praxisflux sweep skill — the orchestrator that runs many tasks through development at once — already knew all of this. Its “concurrency doctrine” treats collisions as routine, not exceptional. Here are three of its actual rules:

SKILL — pdlc/skills/sweep/SKILL.md 

- On conflict: **rebase** (never merge-commit into a task branch), take main's side for
  anything you didn't deliberately change, then re-run gates — a rebase can silently
  invalidate a design-doc amendment or a pinned reference.
- Two PRs heavy in the same hotspot must not merge within one re-ground cycle without a
  rebase between them, or the grounding gates thrash.
- When your open PR conflicts with a sibling session's, let the **smaller** one merge
  first, regardless of whose it is.
PLAIN ENGLISH

When your work collides with main, replay your changes on top of the newest version — never mash the two histories together — and where you didn’t mean to differ, let main win…

…then re-run every check, because moving your work onto a new base can quietly break things that passed before…

…like a documentation note that was pinned to the old version and is now out of date.

Two batches of changes that hit the same busy files must not land back to back without…

…refreshing one against the other first — otherwise the documentation checks flip-flop endlessly.

And when your proposal collides with a teammate’s, the smaller one goes first…

…no matter whose it is — small changes are cheap to replay on top of big ones, not the reverse.

Good rules — but notice what they all have in common: they tell you what to do after you discover trouble, or they rely on the session remembering to check. Written doctrine only works if every session reads it, every time. The next module is about one project’s answer: stop relying on memory, and put a machine at the door.
